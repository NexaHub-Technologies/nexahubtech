# PC Basics

The course that assumes nothing. Where the parts are, what the machine is
actually doing, and how to use one without the constant worry of breaking it.

> **The mental model — Everything lives somewhere**
>
> A computer never puts anything nowhere. Every document you save, every photo
> you copy, every app you install goes to a place that has a name — and that
> name is a path you can read out loud. Beginners lose work because they believe
> the machine decides where things go. It does not: it asks, and the answer is
> usually a box that got clicked past. Once you can say where a thing is, you can
> find it again, copy it, back it up and share it — and the fear goes with the
> not knowing.

**Why in person, and not online like the rest.** Every other course here is
taught live online. This one cannot be: the first thing a student needs is
someone standing beside them saying *that one, on the left*. Sitting a person who
has never used a computer in front of a video call is asking them to use the
skill the course exists to teach.

## Before you start

**Know:** nothing about computers. That is not a figure of speech — this is the
course that assumes none. You need to read and write in English. If you already
use a smartphone, you are further ahead than you think, and a fair amount of
week one will be putting names to things you already do.

**Have:** a laptop running Windows, macOS or Linux — or use one of ours in the
centre, which is what most people do. A phone that can receive SMS, for setting
up accounts safely. A USB drive of any size. 4–5 hours a week outside sessions
to practise, and the practice is the course.

> **On pace.** Six weeks is not long, and the thing that decides whether it
> works is not clever teaching — it is whether you touch a computer between
> sessions. Twenty minutes a day beats four hours on a Saturday. Nobody has ever
> learnt this by watching.

> **On breaking it.** You will be told this in week one and it is worth reading
> now: on a school machine there is almost nothing you can click that we cannot
> undo. The hesitation is the only thing that actually slows people down, and it
> is the first thing the course sets out to remove.

## Module 01 — The machine, and what it is doing
*Week 1*

The parts, the desktop, the mouse and the keyboard — and why nothing you click
can break it.

**By the end you can**
- Name the parts of the machine you are using and say what each one is for.
- Start up, shut down and restart properly, and say why pulling the plug is
  different.
- Work a window: move it, resize it, close it, and find the one that went away.
- Type without hunting for every letter, and use the handful of keyboard
  shortcuts that pay for themselves in a week.

**The misconception this clears.** That the computer might break if you click the
wrong thing, so the safe move is to touch as little as possible. This is the
single biggest brake on a beginner and it is backwards: the machine is designed
to be poked at, almost everything is reversible, and the people who get good are
the ones who click things to find out.

**Exercise brief.** Working in pairs, break things on purpose. Close a window
without saving and get the work back. Minimise an application until it seems
gone, then find it. Change the desktop background, then put it back exactly as it
was. Fill a page with text and undo all of it, then redo it. Write down, for each
one, what you expected to happen and what actually happened.

**Model answer demonstrates.** Recovery in each case, and the reasoning said
plainly — "it is not gone, it is minimised, and here is where the machine put
it". The instructor's own list of what they got wrong the first time is read out;
this is deliberate.

## Module 02 — Files, folders and where things go
*Week 2*

Paths, saving, naming, copying and backing up. The module the whole course is
built on.

**By the end you can**
- Read a path out loud and say what each part of it means.
- Save a file **where you meant to**, and find it again a week later.
- Tell copying from moving, and say which one just happened.
- Make a backup and — the part almost everyone skips — restore from it.

**The misconception this clears.** That the computer decides where things go, so
"where did it save?" is an unanswerable question. It never decides. It asks,
usually in a box with a folder name at the top, and the answer was on screen at
the moment it was clicked past. This module is the mental model of the whole
course, which is why it gets a week to itself in a six-week course.

**Exercise brief.** You are given a file and a location, written as a path, and
you must save it there — no clicking around afterwards to check. Then the reverse:
given ten files, write the full path of each **before** opening any folder. Then
the awkward one: save a file, and without moving it, say three different ways of
getting back to it.

**Model answer demonstrates.** Paths read as directions rather than recited as
spells, and the save dialog treated as a question being asked rather than an
obstacle in the way.

## Module 03 — Documents, numbers and slides
*Weeks 3–4*

Write a letter properly, make a spreadsheet do the arithmetic, and build a deck
worth showing.

**By the end you can**
- Structure a document with heading styles, so it can be restyled, contented and
  navigated without being retyped.
- Write a formula that refers to other cells, and know why that is different from
  typing the answer in.
- Build a chart that says one thing clearly.
- Export to PDF and know why you would.

**The misconception this clears.** That formatting is decoration applied at the
end. Bold-and-bigger *looks* like a heading and is not one — it carries no
structure, so nothing downstream can use it, and every change becomes manual
again. The same mistake in the spreadsheet is typing a total you worked out
yourself: it is correct exactly once, and then it silently is not.

**Exercise brief.** Two documents are supplied, and they look identical on
screen. One is built with styles and formulas; the other is built by hand.
Without being told which is which, find out — then make a change that proves it.
Afterwards, take the hand-built one and repair it.

**Model answer demonstrates.** The test found by changing something rather than
by looking: switch the theme, change an input. The repair done by applying
structure rather than by retyping the document.

## Module 04 — The internet, email and accounts
*Week 5*

Browse, search well, judge what you find, and set up an account you can get back
into.

**By the end you can**
- Tell the address bar from the search box, and read a web address well enough to
  know whose site you are on.
- Search with terms that narrow rather than terms that describe.
- Set up an email account with two-factor authentication and a recovery route.
- Attach a file, and know what you have just handed over.

**The misconception this clears.** That the first result is the answer, and that
being on the internet makes a claim true. Also its quieter twin: that an account
is something you make once and never think about, rather than a door with a key
you must not lose.

**Exercise brief.** You are given three questions with genuinely contested
answers online — the sort of thing a market trader might need settled. Find each
answer, then produce the **three sources you used and one line on why you trusted
each**. One of the three questions has a plausible, popular, wrong answer in the
first page of results.

**Model answer demonstrates.** Sources judged by who published them and what they
had to gain, not by how confident the page sounded. The planted wrong answer
identified, with the reason it was convincing.

## Module 05 — Staying safe, and getting unstuck
*Week 6*

Scams, passwords and privacy — plus how to describe a problem well enough to be
helped.

**By the end you can**
- Spot the tells in a scam message, and name them rather than just sense them.
- Use passwords that do not depend on your memory or your birthday.
- Say what a website, an app or a form is actually asking you for.
- Describe a problem to someone who can fix it, in a way that gets it fixed.

**The misconception this clears.** That a scam is obvious, and that being careful
is a matter of being clever. The good ones are not obvious and being clever is no
defence — what works is a small number of habits that do not depend on you being
alert on the day. And on the other side: that asking for help is an admission.
"It doesn't work" is not a request for help; a description is.

**Exercise brief.** Two parts, both from your own life rather than from a
worksheet. First, a scam file: three messages you have **actually received**,
each annotated with **the specific tell** — and names, numbers and account
details blacked out before you hand it in. Second, take something on a computer
that has gone wrong for you recently and write the request for help you should
have sent: what you were doing, what you expected, what happened instead, and
what you already tried.

**Model answer demonstrates.** Tells named precisely rather than sensed — "the
address is not the bank's, it is a lookalike with a dash in it" is a tell; "it
looked fake" is not. And a help request somebody could act on without asking a
single follow-up question.

## Assessment across the course

No grades, and no course project. This course is assessed entirely through the
per-module exercise briefs above — for a six-week course at this level, a
portfolio piece is not what anybody needs. Competence is, and the exercises are
where it shows.

Module 02 is the one that decides everything: if paths make sense by the end of
week two, the rest of the course is downhill, and if they do not, no amount of
practice with spreadsheets will cover it. Anyone still guessing at where files
went in week three repeats that week, on their own, before module 03 starts.
